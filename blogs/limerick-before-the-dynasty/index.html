There are sports where supporters know, sooner or later, the wait will end. And there are sports where that feeling quietly fades after thirty years. Limerick hurling was the second kind. For every fan who grew up in the eighties and nineties, the pattern was familiar: a good season, a run through Munster, sometimes the final, and then the familiar disappointment on Croke Park's September turf.

And then, in 2018, everything at once.

But you only really understand the dynasty when you know what came before it. What it meant to wait decade after decade for a title that always seemed just out of reach. And why a county like Limerick - proud, steeped in hurling tradition, larger in the GAA world than most people outside Ireland realise - took as long as it did.

Part One - The Long Wait

What Limerick Hurling Looked Like Before 2018

The short answer: 45 years without an All-Ireland. From 1973 to 2018. But the number alone doesn't tell the story. Because in those 45 years, Limerick reached five All-Ireland finals - and lost every single one. 1974, 1980, 1994, 1996, and a semi-final in 2007 that felt like another final slipping away. Almost winning is, in a certain way, harder than not being there at all.

The 1973 team had itself waited 33 years for that title - Limerick hadn't won since 1940. When Eamonn Grimes lifted the Liam McCarthy Cup that September, it felt like a new era beginning. What followed instead was something more complicated: a county that kept producing good teams and kept finding new ways to fall short.

The Drought in Detail

Five Finals. Five Defeats. One County.

These are not the years of a county that simply wasn't there. These are the years of a county that was almost there, every single time.

1973
All-Ireland Champions

The Last Title - For 45 Years

Limerick beat Kilkenny at Croke Park. Eamonn Grimes as captain, Richie Bennis as top scorer, Éamonn Cregan repositioning himself from forward to centre-back to neutralise the Kilkenny attack. A generation of players seizing their moment after years of near-misses - and ending a drought that had already stretched back to 1940. It was their seventh All-Ireland. No one knew it would be their last for nearly half a century.

1974
All-Ireland Final - Defeat

Back the Year After. Beaten by Kilkenny.

The momentum of the title was still there, but Kilkenny took it back. The wait that had just ended in celebration begins again, quietly, in the days after the final whistle.

1980
All-Ireland Final - Defeat

Galway, a Misplaced Pass, and What Might Have Been

Defeat to Galway in the final. Éamonn Cregan, by now a legend of the game, later admitted that a misplayed pass in the closing minutes may have cost them the match. "If I had hand-passed the ball properly, we might have survived," he said years later. That kind of sentence stays in a county's memory for a long time.

1994
All-Ireland Final - Defeat

The Offaly Comeback - The Most Painful Day

Limerick reach the final for the first time in 14 years. They lead for most of the game. Offaly score 2-5 in the final seven minutes. Limerick lose by 3-16 to 2-13. Offaly are managed by none other than Éamonn Cregan - the man who played for Limerick in 1973, now standing on the other side of the divide. For a generation of supporters, this afternoon defines everything that follows.

1996
All-Ireland Final - Defeat

Fourteen Against Fifteen. Still Lost.

The most difficult to explain. Wexford have a man sent off in the 34th minute. Limerick play the entire second half with a numerical advantage. They score four points in that half. Wexford win. Manager Tom Ryan said afterwards: "The occasion got to them." It is the kind of defeat that says something about psychology rather than talent - and that is harder to fix than any tactical problem.

2007
All-Ireland Final - Defeat

Back Via the Back Door. Kilkenny Win. Again.

Limerick reach the final via the qualifier system without winning Munster. They face Kilkenny, who are building toward their own four-in-a-row dynasty. Defeat by 2-19 to 1-15. It will be eleven years before Limerick see another final. Eleven years that turn out to be exactly what the county needed.

2018
All-Ireland Champions - The Dynasty Begins

45 Years Ends on a September Sunday

A young Limerick side - built largely from the under-21 All-Ireland winners of 2015 - beats Galway 3-16 to 2-18 in the final. The Liam McCarthy Cup returns to Limerick after 45 years. Manager John Kiely, in the changing room after the final whistle: "Is that the best they'll ever be? No - they'll be better." He was right.

Part Two - Why It Matters

Hurling is Not Professional Sport. That Is the Point.

Here is what people outside Ireland find difficult to understand about the GAA: it is not professional sport. The hurler who lines out for Limerick at Croke Park on a Sunday has a regular job on Monday. There are no transfer fees, no agents, no global broadcast deals. You play for your county because that is where you are from. That is the only thing that counts.

That makes the waiting different from anything you'd experience following a professional club. These aren't players who can be replaced with a transfer window signing. They are people from your community - the son of someone you know, the brother of your neighbour. When they lose, the county loses. And when they finally win, it is felt across an entire region in a way that professional sport rarely manages to replicate.

The Munster Championship is the most charged environment within all of that. Limerick, Cork, Tipperary, Clare, Waterford - five counties with a deep historical distrust of one another on the hurling field, playing it out again every summer. Limerick versus Tipperary carries the same weight as a Clásico. Just without the merchandise.

"Hurling is the fastest field sport in the world, played by amateurs. You can't understand what that means until you see it live."

- James, Beyond the Pitch guide, Kilkenny

Part Three - The Renaissance

The Architects of the Dynasty - John Kiely and the Making of a Team

When John Kiely took charge in 2016, Limerick had gone through a difficult stretch. The 2007 final had raised hopes that were not sustained. Kiely - a former Limerick player, calm and methodical - approached the rebuild differently. Working alongside performance coach Paul Kinnerk, he built a system around a group of players who had already proven something at underage level.

The 2015 under-21 All-Ireland was the foundation. Several of those players - Cian Lynch, Declan Hannon, Kyle Hayes - would become the core of the senior team that won four consecutive All-Irelands. Lynch was named Hurler of the Year in 2018 at twenty-one years old. The 45-year wait hadn't weakened the county. It had made them understand, more clearly than most, what they were playing for.

John Kiely

Manager, 2016–present

Former Limerick player who brought a new mental and tactical discipline to the setup. Credited alongside Kinnerk for reshaping the team's entire culture of preparation and resilience.

Paul Kinnerk

Performance Coach

The quiet architect of the team's tactical evolution. Introduced a structured system that gave Limerick an edge in physicality and decision-making that other counties have spent years trying to replicate.

Cian Lynch

Named All-Ireland Hurler of the Year in 2018 at twenty-one. A central midfielder with a reading of the game that seems several seconds ahead of everyone else on the field.

Declan Hannon

Captain, 2018 & 2020

The captain who lifted the Liam McCarthy Cup in 2018, ending the 45-year wait. A composed centre-back who became the embodiment of the team's measured, relentless approach.

Part Four - For the Traveller

Why You Need Context to Really Feel This Sport

Hurling is a sport you can watch without any background knowledge and experience as an impressive athletic spectacle. But you'll miss the half that matters.

The half you miss is exactly what's at stake. Why a Munster Championship match in June carries more weight for someone from Limerick than any ordinary fixture. Why the players after the match sit in the same pub as the supporters. Why a county of fewer than 200,000 people became the dominant hurling force of an entire decade.

That is the difference between attending a match and being inside one. And for outsiders who genuinely want to understand this, physical presence with the right context is the only way in. It is not the kind of thing that translates on a screen.

Questions & Answers

Everything You Wanted to Know About Limerick Hurling

How long was Limerick's hurling drought before their modern All-Ireland successes? +
Limerick won their last All-Ireland Senior Hurling Championship in 1973. What followed were 45 years of lost finals, provincial heartbreak and painful near-misses before the county lifted the cup again in 2018. Those 45 years form the emotional foundation beneath the dynasty that followed, and are deeply embedded in Limerick GAA folklore.
Which All-Ireland finals did Limerick lose during their decades of drought? +
Limerick lost All-Ireland finals in 1974 (against Kilkenny), 1980 (against Galway), 1994 (against Offaly, in one of the most dramatic finals in GAA history) and 1996 (against Wexford). Each defeat dug the collective trauma deeper - particularly 1994, when Limerick were favourites and Offaly came back from seemingly nowhere in the final seven minutes.
What made the 1994 All-Ireland hurling final such a turning point in Limerick GAA history? +
In 1994, Limerick lost the All-Ireland final to Offaly by 3-16 to 2-13, a match in which Limerick had led for most of the game. Offaly's late comeback is widely regarded as one of the most painful reversals in GAA history. The fact that Offaly were managed by Éamonn Cregan - a Limerick legend who had played in the 1973 final - made it feel almost theatrical in its cruelty.
Who are the architects of the modern Limerick hurling renaissance that began in 2018? +
Manager John Kiely and performance coach Paul Kinnerk are widely recognised as the key architects. Kiely, a former Limerick player himself, brought a new mental and tactical discipline to the setup. The playing core - Cian Lynch (All-Ireland Hurler of the Year 2018 and 2021), Declan Hannon, Aaron Gillane and Kyle Hayes - formed one of the most dominant squads the game has seen.
Why is Limerick so good at hurling - what changed? +
The turning point was the under-21 All-Ireland win in 2015, which produced a generation of players who knew how to win at the highest level. When John Kiely and Paul Kinnerk built a senior structure around that group, they combined youth and confidence with a systematic approach to preparation that most counties hadn't encountered. The 45-year wait, paradoxically, may have sharpened that hunger.
