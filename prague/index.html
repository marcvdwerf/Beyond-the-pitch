Most people come to Prague for the castle. The Charles Bridge. A few cold beers in a cellar bar. All of that is real -but it's the surface.

Beneath the cobblestones is a city that has been occupied, silenced, and rebuilt -twice in one century. And somewhere in the middle of all of it, a sport became a weapon. Not a metaphor for one. An actual weapon -a way for a small country to look a superpower in the eye and refuse to look away.

Walk the old city with Kamil -a Prague local and world traveller -through the exact streets where it happened. Hear things that don't make the guidebooks. And then, if you're here in season, end the evening exactly where the story ends: watching Czechs play ice hockey live.

No prior knowledge needed. Groups from 2 people. Prague is one of Europe's most connected cities -direct flights from London, Amsterdam, Berlin, Paris and beyond. A natural stop on any Europe trip.

01

National Theatre Courtyard

A nation is born -and picks up a stick

1918. Czechoslovakia declares independence after centuries of Habsburg rule. Within a generation, ice hockey is everywhere -in frozen ponds, schoolyards, the national psyche. This is where Kamil explains why a sport becomes more than a sport, and what it costs a country when that sport is taken away.

02

Žofín River Island

The first rink -playing the Nazis on ice

Prague's original hockey stadium stood on this island. In 1938, Czechoslovakia faced Nazi Germany in a match that was never just about sport. Violent, politically loaded, played in front of a country that knew what was coming. Months later, they were gone -occupied without a shot fired. The rink outlasted the republic.

03

Zlatá Hospůdka

The night they arrested the champions

1950. The communists have been in power for two years. The national hockey team -Olympic medalists, world champions -are arrested by the secret police and sentenced to decades in prison and forced labour. No trial. No reason given. A generation of talent wiped out overnight. The Soviet machine now had a clear path to dominance. Kamil pauses here. This is the heaviest stop on the route.

A small Czech beer here, if you want one. About €2. Some stories are easier with something in your hand.
04

Myslíkova Street

When the state turned sport into a show

Under communism, sport became propaganda. The Spartakiáda -mass gymnastic displays performed by hundreds of thousands -were not voluntary. Kamil's own father took part in 1975. Standing in a field with 180,000 others, moving in perfect unison, whether he wanted to or not. A personal story, in a country full of them.

05

Lucerna Palace

The palace that was almost a hockey arena

One of Prague's great what-ifs. Lucerna was originally designed as an ice hockey stadium. It became something else entirely. Kamil takes you inside -a brief break, a drink if you want one, and the kind of detail that only a local knows to tell you. Not every stop needs to be heavy.

06

Wenceslas Square

Tanks, then goals -1968 invasion and the 1969 riots

In August 1968, Soviet tanks rolled into this square. Then, eight months later, Czechoslovakia beat the USSR at ice hockey. Twice. The crowd that spilled onto this square wasn't celebrating a sport. They were reclaiming something that had been taken from them. The riots that followed led to direct political changes. A hockey match that rewrote the country.

07

Politických Vězňů Street

The Velvet Revolution -and the end of a shared country

The current Communist Party headquarters sits here -still operating, still on this street. Kamil explains the Velvet Revolution of 1989: how a country said enough, peacefully, and meant it. And then how Czechoslovakia split into two nations -two flags, two stories, two hockey teams. The shared chapter was over.

08

Czech Ice Hockey Hall of Fame

1998. Nagano. The goal that stopped a country.

You've walked the whole story. Now you see where it ends. The Hall of Fame is interactive, fully English, built around the moments that defined Czech hockey: the 1998 Olympic Gold in Nagano, the Golden Hat-Trick of 1999, 2000, and 2001. Hašek. Jágr. The trophies are real. The footage is right in front of you. By this point, you'll understand exactly why it all mattered.

Frequently asked questions

Good to know

Do I need to know anything about ice hockey before I join? +

Nothing at all. Most people who join this tour have never watched a hockey match in their lives. That's fine -the sport makes sense once you understand the context. Kamil builds the whole thing from the ground up.

When is the hockey season? +

The Czech Extraliga runs September through April. Match tickets for Sparta Praha or HC Slavia Praha are included in the Version B price. Outside of season, Version A - the walking tour and Hall of Fame -runs year-round and is a complete experience in its own right.

How does the booking process work? +

Send us your preferred date, group size, and which version interests you. We review each request personally within 24–48 hours, confirm Kamil's availability and match access where relevant, then send you next steps to finalise. We don't take bookings on autopilot -everything is checked before it's confirmed.

Is the history heavy? Is it appropriate for all ages? +

The history is real and Kamil doesn't sanitise it. The Nazi occupation and communist arrests are central to the story. He presents it with honesty and human perspective, not shock. Teens and adults will find it compelling. Younger children are welcome with some parental context around the heavier moments.

How is this different from a regular Prague walking tour? +

Most Prague tours take you to the same five landmarks and call it history. This one uses sport as the thread -and sport turns out to be the most honest way to understand a culture. You'll stand on Wenceslas Square and actually understand what happened there. That's a different kind of tour.

What if I'm visiting Prague as part of a longer Europe trip? +

Prague is one of Europe's most accessible cities -direct trains from Berlin, Vienna, and Munich; direct flights from London, Amsterdam, Paris, and most major hubs. The tour works perfectly as a two-hour add-on to an evening in the city. If you can be in Prague for a weekend, you have more than enough time.
